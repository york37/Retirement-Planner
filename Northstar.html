Annual withdrawals and cash flow

The current age is your age at January 1, 2026. Every year through the final planning age is included. At retirement, the entered annual RRSP withdrawal begins as a fixed nominal gross payment, capped by available registered assets. Enter 0 for no voluntary payment. It is not automatically increased to fund tax or depleted other accounts. Required RRIF minimums override a lower entry: the annual registered draw is the larger of your entry and the legal minimum, never their sum. RRSP converts internally at the end of age 71 with no taxable cash payment; the first RRIF minimum is at age 72, even if still working.

Pension, CPP/OAS, the scheduled registered payment and cash distributions fund retirement expenses and attributable tax. Non-registered capital sales fill any remaining gap, followed by TFSA withdrawals. Tax and reduced distributions from the units sold are solved together. If these sources are insufficient, a spending shortfall or unpaid tax is shown; the manual registered amount is not exceeded. Excess cash is reinvested in non-registered assets at year-end. No discretionary sales occur before retirement; investment-income taxes while working remain external, preserving entered net savings. Tax-context income only sets brackets and is not spending cash. The graph colours and source badges identify actual capital sales and registered payments; distributions appear separately in the table and tooltips.

Returns and distributions

Capital withdrawals are modeled at the beginning of the year, followed by growth on retained assets. Cash distributions are estimated on the non-registered balance remaining after those sales. As an annual budgeting approximation, that year’s distributions can fund that year’s expenses; monthly payment timing is not simulated. Equity cash distributions = retained balance × equity allocation × entered distribution yield. Exactly 50% is assumed Canadian eligible dividends and 50% foreign ordinary income. Positive bond return is modeled as cash interest; a negative bond return is a price loss with no negative interest. These cash flows are part of the entered total returns. Non-registered price return is total return minus distributions, preventing double counting. TFSA/RRSP returns remain inside their accounts. Surplus cash and fixed nominal savings are invested at year-end, earning returns from the following year. Savings end before retirement; RRSP savings after age 71 redirect to non-registered assets.

Three account tax treatments

TFSA: growth and withdrawals are tax free. RRSP/RRIF: returns are sheltered and full cash withdrawals are ordinary taxable income; progressive final tax is used rather than withholding. Non-registered: the initial balance defaults to 70% cost basis and 30% unrealized gain, editable and saved locally. Sales remove proportional cost basis; returned principal is not taxable. Half of net realized capital gains enters income. Realized losses offset capital gains only and carry forward. Price appreciation changes the gain percentage. Contributions and reinvested cash increase cost basis; distributions spent on living costs or tax do not.

Canadian eligible dividends

Actual eligible dividend cash is grossed up by 38%: $1,000 cash adds $1,380 to taxable income. Federal dividend credit is 15.0198% of that taxable amount and Ontario credit is 10%. These credits are non-refundable: unused credit cannot create negative tax or offset Ontario health premium or OAS recovery. The Ontario credit is deducted after surtax and before the Ontario tax reduction, following Form ON428. The gross-up also affects age-credit phase-outs and OAS recovery. Remaining foreign distributions and bond interest are fully taxable without dividend credits. The fixed 50% eligible share is a portfolio assumption, not a claim that half of every investment’s dividends qualify. Foreign withholding and foreign tax credits are excluded. Table/CSV dividend credit columns show calculated entitlements, which may exceed usable credits at low income.

Pensions, inflation and taxes

Pension starts at the entered age; CPP and OAS start at 65. Their annual inputs and living expenses are in today’s dollars, indexed from 2026 through the final year. The pension is treated as an eligible lifetime employer pension; CPP/OAS do not qualify for pension credits. OAS recovery is 15% above the 2026 threshold of $95,323, capped at OAS received, deducted from income and added to payable using same-year settlement. OAS age-75 increases, early/late start adjustments and benefit eligibility are not estimated. Other-income context excludes these benefits and stays outside spending cash.

Federal/Ontario brackets, basic personal credits, federal BPA taper, age/pension credits, Ontario surtax, basic reduction and health premium are included. Future thresholds and modeled credits are uniformly indexed with the inflation option or frozen; rates stay at 2026 levels. This is a planning assumption, not a forecast of legislation. Refundable benefits including GIS, CPP/EI premiums, AMT, fees, estate/probate and final deemed-disposition taxes are excluded. Personal contribution room is not enforced; verify your TFSA/RRSP room. RRSP deduction savings are reported externally and are not reinvested.

Reading the projection

Balances are year-end values after cash withdrawals, growth, reinvestment and contributions. Real balances use (1 + inflation)years since 2026 + 1. The effective rate is total federal/Ontario tax plus OAS recovery divided by taxable income, including the eligible-dividend gross-up and tax-context income. The other-income context tax stays external; only incremental benefit/investment tax is funded by plan cash. A dash means no taxable income. Both graphs start at retirement with five-year labels; all annual values remain available. Tax tabs show effective rate or nominal/real payable. Annual table and CSV include working years, account balances, distributions, taxes and actual withdrawals.

Historical returns, used carefully

The default return assumptions use the 2020 Global Investment Returns Yearbook, published by London Business School: global annualized real equity return of 5.2% and bond return of 2.0% over 1900–2019. The initial nominal returns of 7.304% for equities and 4.04% for bonds are derived as (1 + historical real return) × 1.02 − 1, using 2% inflation. All three rate inputs are editable. Inflation changes the purchasing-power and expense calculations; it does not automatically change your chosen nominal returns. These are global historical benchmarks, not observed nominal CAD returns or forecasts. Currency movements and hedging are not modeled. Overwrite the return boxes to use your own nominal assumptions.

The 2026 UBS Yearbook was also reviewed; its public announcement quotes US asset-class returns, which are not substituted for global defaults here. Constant returns do not capture volatility, sequence-of-returns risk or the probability of success. “Funded” means only that this specific scenario covers the modeled spending.
